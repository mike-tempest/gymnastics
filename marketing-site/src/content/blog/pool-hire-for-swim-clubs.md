Ask most swim club treasurers what keeps them awake and the answer is rarely the affiliation invoice or the insurance renewal. It is the water. Pool hire is the single biggest cost line for the vast majority of UK clubs, and it is also the one the committee feels least able to control. The rate goes up when the operator says it goes up, the good slots are always in demand, and a club can spend years quietly accepting whatever it is offered.

It does not have to work that way. **Pool hire** is a negotiation like any other, and clubs that treat it as one, rather than as a bill that simply arrives, tend to pay a fairer rate and hold onto their water more securely. This guide sets out how to negotiate pool hire and lane time for your swim club: who you are actually dealing with, the levers that move a rate, what your agreement should cover, and what to do when the number is simply too high.

## The short answer: how to negotiate pool hire for your swim club

Find out who runs the pool and what they care about. Work out your real cost per lane hour and how well you fill the water you already pay for. Go in with something to offer, usually reliable custom that fills slots the operator struggles to sell, and ask for a rate that reflects it. Get the rate, the notice period for any change, and your priority for next season in writing. And know your alternatives before you sit down, because the club that has nowhere else to go has nothing to negotiate with.

## Why pool time is the hardest cost your club faces

Every other cost in a club budget has some give in it. You can shop around for insurance, adjust coaching hours, or cut back on equipment for a season. Pool time is different for three reasons.

First, it is usually the largest line by a distance, so a small percentage change in the rate moves the whole budget. Our [budget planning guide](/blog/swim-club-budget-planning-guide/) explains where pool hire typically sits against everything else you spend.

Second, water is scarce. Public pool provision in many areas has shrunk, and the surviving pools are heavily booked, so clubs are often price-takers competing with swimming lessons, casual swimming and other clubs for the same lanes.

Third, losing your slot is close to existential. A club can survive a difficult season financially, but a club with nowhere to train cannot function at all. That imbalance is exactly why the relationship with your pool operator deserves as much committee attention as the accounts do.

## Know who you are actually negotiating with

The biggest mistake clubs make is treating "the pool" as one thing. Who owns and runs it changes what will persuade them.

- **A council-run pool.** The local authority operates it directly. Decisions can be slower and more rules-bound, but there is often a genuine remit to support community sport and junior participation that a well-run club can appeal to.
- **A leisure trust.** Many councils have handed operation to a charitable leisure trust. Trusts have to balance the books, but their charitable objects usually include community health and access, which is a real argument for a club that gets local children swimming.
- **A private leisure operator.** Councils frequently contract a commercial operator to run their centres. These operators are measured on income per available hour, so your case has to be commercial: you fill water that would otherwise earn nothing.
- **A school, college, university or private pool.** Often overlooked, these can have spare evening and weekend capacity and an interest in community links or extra income, and are worth approaching if the public pool is full or expensive.

Before any conversation, find out which of these you are dealing with and who actually holds the pen on pricing. The named person who can say yes is rarely the duty manager on the poolside.

## Do your homework before you ask for anything

Walking in and asking for a discount rarely works. Walking in knowing your own numbers does.

- **Know your cost per lane hour.** Divide what you pay by the lane hours you actually receive. This, not the headline rate, is the figure to compare between options and to track year on year.
- **Know your utilisation.** Are you paying for eight lanes and filling five? A club that is honest about this can sometimes trade unused lanes back for a lower bill without losing anything it was really using.
- **Know your growth plan.** If you expect to add a squad or a session next year, that is extra business for the operator. Bring it to the table rather than asking for it piecemeal later.
- **Know your alternatives.** The single most powerful thing in any negotiation is a credible option to walk away. Quietly find out what other water exists locally, what it costs, and whether it could take you. You may never use it, but knowing it exists changes the conversation.

## The levers that actually move a pool hire rate

You are unlikely to argue an operator down purely on the grounds that the club is short of money. What works is giving them something they value in return.

- **Volume and commitment.** A longer booking, more hours, or a multi-year agreement gives the operator certainty, and certainty is worth a better unit rate. Ask what a bigger or longer commitment would unlock.
- **Take the awkward slots.** Early mornings, late evenings and off-peak weekend hours are hard for operators to sell. A club willing to fill that dead water is doing the operator a favour, and off-peak rates are usually lower anyway.
- **Be the client they never worry about.** Pay on time, never overrun, leave the facility as you found it, and give plenty of notice for any change. Operators quietly protect the hirers who cause them no trouble, and that goodwill is worth real money at renewal.
- **Make the community case where it lands.** With a council or trust, the number of local children you get swimming, your work with schools, and your role in the [club's wider health and participation](/blog/club-health-tracker-guide-swim-england-2026/) are legitimate arguments, not sob stories. Put them in writing.

Swim England's facilities team publishes guidance for those who manage and plan pools, and understanding how operators think about their water helps you frame a request in terms they respond to. You can find their work through the [Swim England facilities pages](https://www.swimming.org/swimengland/the-swim-england-facilities-team/).

## Get the agreement in writing

A handshake rate is where mid-season surprises come from. Whatever you agree, get a written hire agreement or licence that spells out the following.

- **The rate, and the notice you get before it changes.** This is the clause that stops a rise landing with no warning. Even a simple commitment to give one term's notice of a rate change lets you plan and, if needed, [raise fees](/blog/how-to-raise-swim-club-fees/) in an orderly way rather than scrambling.
- **Cancellation notice, both ways.** How much notice must you give to drop a slot, and, just as importantly, how much must the operator give you to take one away?
- **What happens if the pool closes.** Plant failure, maintenance and unplanned closures happen. Agree in advance whether you are charged, offered alternative water, or refunded.
- **Lifeguard cover.** Establish clearly whether lifeguards are included in the rate or whether the club must provide and pay for its own qualified cover. This has real cost and safety implications.
- **Term dates and holiday closures.** Know which weeks you are paying for and which the pool shuts, so your fee model matches the sessions members actually get.
- **Priority for next season.** First refusal on your existing slots is one of the most valuable things you can secure. It is far easier to agree when you are already the reliable incumbent than to win it back after losing it.

Keep the signed agreement with the [committee handover file](/blog/swimming-club-committee-handover-checklist/). The next treasurer and the next chair should never have to reconstruct the pool deal from memory.

## Protecting your water for the long term

The rate matters, but security of tenure matters more. Aim to be the hirer the operator would least like to lose. That means one named person on the committee owning the relationship, turning up to any user-group meetings the centre runs, flagging problems early rather than letting them fester, and honouring the agreement to the letter. A club that is easy to deal with and visibly valuable to the community is the last one an operator moves on when water gets tight.

## When the rate is simply unaffordable

Sometimes the number really is beyond what the club can carry, and no amount of goodwill closes the gap. You still have options before you either accept a loss-making rate or fold sessions.

- **Shift sessions to cheaper water.** Move whatever you can to off-peak hours, or to a cheaper pool for the sessions that do not need your best slot.
- **Share with another club.** Two local clubs filling lanes together can sometimes secure a block rate neither could justify alone. Collaboration beats competing for the same scarce water.
- **Reduce and restructure, do not just trim.** If you are paying for lanes you do not fill, restructure the timetable so squads share sessions sensibly rather than paying for half-empty water.
- **Look beyond the public pool.** School, college and private pools can have spare capacity, especially in the evenings and at weekends.
- **Fund access where it fits.** Some grants and funders specifically support facility access and widening participation. The [grants and funding guide](/blog/swim-club-grants-and-funding-uk/) covers where to look.

And if the cost is genuinely fixed, the sessions are essential, and the club cannot absorb it, the honest response is to build it into a planned, clearly explained fee increase rather than run down reserves in silence. That is a committee decision made in the open, not a problem to hide.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does it cost to hire a pool for a swim club?

There is no national rate. What you pay depends on who runs the pool, whether the water is peak or off-peak, and whether you are hiring whole lanes exclusively or sharing the pool. The figure that matters for your budget is the cost per lane hour, not the headline hourly rate, because a cheaper pool that only offers you half the lanes can work out dearer per swimmer. Always get next season's rate confirmed in writing before you set fees, rather than assuming this year's figure will hold.

### Who owns the pool my swim club hires?

Most public pools in the UK are owned by the local authority, but they are often run day to day by a leisure trust or a private leisure operator under a contract, rather than by the council directly. Others are school, college, university or private pools. Each type has different priorities: a trust or council usually has community-use aims you can appeal to, while a commercial operator is focused on income per lane hour. Find out which you are dealing with before you open a conversation, because it changes what will persuade them.

### How can a swim club get a better pool hire rate?

Give the operator something they value. Commit to a longer booking or more hours for a better unit rate, offer to take the unpopular off-peak slots that would otherwise sit empty, and be the reliable, low-maintenance client who pays on time and never leaves the changing rooms in a state. Filling water that would otherwise earn nothing is the strongest card most clubs hold. Where the pool is run by a trust or the council, the club's community and junior-participation value is a genuine argument, not just a favour.

### What should a pool hire agreement include?

Put it in writing and make sure it covers the rate and how much notice you get before it changes, the notice period for cancellation by either side, what happens if the pool has to close for plant failure or maintenance, who provides and pays for lifeguards, the term dates and any holiday closures, and whether you have first refusal on your slots for the following season. A clear agreement is what protects you from a sudden mid-season rate rise or losing your water at short notice.

### What can a club do if pool hire becomes unaffordable?

Before accepting a rate you cannot cover, look at the alternatives: move some sessions to cheaper off-peak water, share a pool with another local club to fill lanes together, reduce or restructure hours so you are not paying for lanes you do not fill, or approach a school, college or private pool nearby. If the cost is genuinely fixed and the sessions are essential, the honest answer is to build it into a planned, well-explained fee increase rather than run the club at a loss.
